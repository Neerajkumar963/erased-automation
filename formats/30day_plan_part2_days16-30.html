THE US MILITARY SECRETLY SPRAYED BACTERIA OVER SAN FRANCISCO, EXPOSING 800,000 AMERICAN CITIZENS.
In September 1950, the US Navy conducted a secret biological warfare experiment called Operation Sea-Spray off the coast of San Francisco, California. For six days, military ships sprayed a massive cloud of Serratia marcescens and Bacillus globigii bacteria into the air to track how a pathogen would disperse over a major city. The test exposed the city's 800,000 residents to the aerosolized bacteria. The military claimed the bacteria was harmless, but soon after, local hospitals reported a spike in rare urinary tract infections, and one patient, Edward Nevin, died from a heart infection caused by the bacteria. The military kept the experiment secret for 27 years until Senate hearings declassified the files in 1977. The Nevin family sued the government, but the court ruled the military had immunity for experimental testing.

THE CIA USED HOWARD HUGHES AS A COVER STORY TO SECRETLY RAISE A SOVIET NUCLEAR SUBMARINE FROM THE OCEAN FLOOR.
In August 1974, the CIA deployed the Hughes Glomar Explorer — one of the largest and most sophisticated ships ever built at that time — to the North Pacific. The cover story: eccentric billionaire Howard Hughes was conducting deep-sea manganese nodule mining research for commercial purposes. The real mission: raising the Soviet submarine K-129, which had sunk in 1968 at a depth of 16,500 feet, to recover its nuclear warheads, code books, and cipher equipment. The operation — officially known as Project AZORIAN — represented one of the most ambitious covert operations ever attempted. The CIA's custom-built capture mechanism successfully raised part of the submarine before mechanical failure caused sections to be lost. The operation remained classified until a 1975 press leak forced partial acknowledgment. The full history was declassified in 2010.
