DuPont used a chemical called C8 (PFOA) to manufacture Teflon non-stick coating starting in the 1950s. By the 1980s, internal company studies — later revealed through litigation — showed C8 was contaminating drinking water near its West Virginia plant and appearing in the blood of workers and local residents. DuPont continued using the chemical for roughly 20 more years without informing regulators or the public of what its own scientists had found. A class-action lawsuit eventually forced an independent scientific panel to study 69,000 affected residents, which in 2012 linked C8 exposure to kidney cancer, testicular cancer, and other diseases. DuPont settled related lawsuits for over $670 million in 2017, and a related company paid an additional $4 billion in 2021. This case became the basis for the film "Dark Waters."()

ENGINEERS WARNED NASA THE NIGHT BEFORE LAUNCH. THEY WERE OVERRULED. 7 ASTRONAUTS DIED 73 SECONDS LATER.
On the night of January 27, 1986, engineer Roger Boisjoly and colleagues at contractor Morton Thiokol held an emergency conference call with NASA, warning that the O-ring seals on the Space Shuttle's solid rocket boosters could fail in the unusually cold temperatures forecast for the next morning's launch. They explicitly recommended delaying. NASA managers pressed Thiokol for a different recommendation; Thiokol management reversed the engineers' objection and approved the launch. The next morning, January 28, 1986, Space Shuttle Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after liftoff, killing all 7 crew members, including teacher Christa McAuliffe. The Rogers Commission investigation later confirmed the O-ring failure exactly as the engineers had predicted. Boisjoly faced professional retaliation after testifying about the warnings.

In May 1973, newly appointed CIA Director James Schlesinger ordered every CIA employee to report, in writing, any activity they believed might have violated the agency's legal charter.
