November 6th, 2000. Saddam Hussein announced that Iraq would stop selling oil in US dollars. From that point forward, Iraq's oil — the second-largest proven reserves on earth at the time — would be priced and traded in euros.

The United States government said nothing publicly. [ pause ] Fourteen months later, the September 11th attacks happened. Fourteen months after that, in March 2003, the United States invaded Iraq. The stated reason was weapons of mass destruction. Not one weapon of mass destruction was ever found.

The Senate Intelligence Committee — the United States government's own investigation — concluded in 2004 that the pre-war intelligence assessments were wrong, and that key judgements were not supported by the underlying evidence.

Saddam Hussein took full control of Iraq in 1979. He was a ruthless authoritarian. In 1988, his forces used chemical weapons — nerve agents and mustard gas — against Kurdish civilians in the town of Halabja. Five thousand people were killed in a single day. This is not contested. It is documented, and it is one of the worst acts of mass murder a government has committed against its own people in the modern era.

He also fought an eight-year war against Iran from 1980 to 1988 — a war in which the United States, at the time, supplied him with intelligence, weapons, and diplomatic cover. Donald Rumsfeld, who would later lead the 2003 invasion as Secretary of Defense, personally visited Saddam in 1983 and 1984 as a US envoy, during the period when chemical weapons were being used.

Both things are true. And both things matter for understanding what came next.

To understand why November 2000 matters, you need to understand one thing about how the global economy works. Since 1971, oil has been priced and sold globally in US dollars. Every country that buys oil — every country on earth — must first acquire US dollars. This creates constant, permanent, structural demand for the American currency, regardless of America's actual economic performance. It is the mechanism that allows the United States to carry a national debt of trillions of dollars without its currency collapsing. It is called the petrodollar system.

Saddam Hussein, in November 2000, switched Iraq's oil sales to euros. Not as a symbolic gesture. Iraq was producing two to three million barrels of oil per day under the UN oil-for-food programme. That volume, priced in euros instead of dollars, was enough to constitute a real, functioning alternative. If it worked — if it survived, if it was seen to work — other oil-producing nations might follow.

In June 2003, three months after the US-led invasion, the Coalition Provisional Authority — the US-appointed governing body now running Iraq — switched all Iraqi oil sales back to US dollars. [ pause ] One of the first administrative acts of the post-invasion government was to reverse the November 2000 oil currency decision.

On February 5th, 2003 — six weeks before the invasion — Secretary of State Colin Powell presented the WMD case to the United Nations Security Council. He showed satellite imagery. He played intercepted audio. He said: "My colleagues, every statement I make today is backed up by sources, solid sources." The presentation was watched by the entire world. It was the moment the invasion case was made in public.

Powell later called it "a blot" on his record — his word, in a 2005 interview. Because the sources were not solid. The satellite imagery was misrepresented. The mobile weapons laboratory claim — which Powell described in detail — came from a single source codenamed "Curveball," an Iraqi defector later assessed by German intelligence, who hosted him, as "not credible" and by the CIA's own reporting as unreliable. The CIA knew this before the Powell presentation. The information was used anyway.

The Senate Intelligence Committee's Phase I report, released in July 2004, found that the Intelligence Community's assessments of Iraq's WMD programmes were "not supported by the underlying intelligence reporting." Not exaggerated. Not misread. Not supported. The UK's Chilcot Inquiry, published in 2016 after a seven-year investigation, concluded the invasion was launched before peaceful options had been exhausted and was based on intelligence that was "presented with a certainty that was not justified."

The Iraq War officially lasted from March 2003 to December 2011. Conservative estimates put the Iraqi civilian death toll at between one hundred and fifty thousand and two hundred thousand people. The Lancet — a peer-reviewed medical journal — estimated the figure at over six hundred thousand by 2006, acknowledging significant uncertainty in the methodology.

No weapons of mass destruction were found. The Iraq Survey Group — the US-led team specifically tasked with finding them — concluded in 2004 that Iraq had ended its nuclear, chemical, and biological weapons programmes in 1991 and had not restarted them. Saddam Hussein was captured in December 2003, tried by an Iraqi court, and executed by hanging on December 30th, 2006. He was not tried for the weapons of mass destruction that justified the invasion. He was tried for the 1982 Dujail massacre — a crime from twenty-one years before the war.
